Tax wealth

Tax income

Each slider changes a group’s average federal tax rate (the share of its total income paid in federal taxes). Groups are ranked by family income, richest first. Slide left (below 0) to give a group a tax cut.

Why the size of the group matters

A tax raises money in proportion to what it taxes. These figures come from the same data that drive the sliders.

  • 4 yearsSeizing every dollar all 989 U.S. billionaires own ($8.4 trillion) would cover the 2026 deficit about four times. Then the money would be gone.
  • 55%of all family income goes to the bottom 90% of families. The top 1% receives about 20%.
  • 3 millionbusiness owners with fortunes averaging about $25 million, based on data from The Everywhere Millionaire. Together they hold more than 13 times the wealth of the Forbes 400.
  • $19.6 trillionheld by the rest of the wealthiest 0.1% of households, more than twice the total amount that billionaires hold.

Questions to argue about

  1. Press “Billionaires only,” then push their wealth tax slider to its maximum. How much of the gap is left? What does that tell you about plans that only focus on billionaires?
  2. Find a way to close the whole gap without touching the bottom 90% (the upper middle group or anyone below them). What rates did it take? Do you believe people would pay them?
  3. Economists Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick argue that car dealers, dentists, and other local business owners hold far more combined wealth than billionaires and are more trusted in their communities. Most of them fall in the top 10% by income (the top three income sliders), and only the richest are reached by the “Rest of the wealthiest 0.1%” slider. If you were willing to tax this group more, how much could you raise? Why might these taxes be harder to pass than a tax on billionaires?
  4. Move the “Middle (50th to 80th)” slider up two points. Compare the tax revenue with what each family would pay. Is that a good trade? Who gets to decide?
  5. Switch wealth tax avoidance from “None” to “Most (70%).” How much does your plan shrink? What would the government need to do to keep that from happening?
  6. Under “Your goal,” choose “Add a new program or tax cut.” Pick a proposal from the menu or enter the yearly cost of one you would add, and watch the goal grow. What would it take to pay for it on top of the deficit? Who would you ask to pay?
  7. This page only raises taxes. Would you cut spending too? If so, what would you cut? How would that change who bears the cost?

How this tool works and its limits

  • The math is simple on purpose: Revenue = a group’s income (or wealth) × the tax rate you set. Real estimates from the Congressional Budget Office or the Joint Committee on Taxation model how people change their behavior, how taxes interact, and how the economy responds. Treat every number here as a rough illustration, not a forecast.
  • Avoidance: The avoidance setting removes a share of revenue from the two wealth taxes. Its options come from estimates of Senator Elizabeth Warren’s wealth tax proposal. Economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman assumed 15% avoidance, based on studies of wealth taxes in other countries. The Penn Wharton Budget Model projected $4.8 trillion over 10 years with no avoidance, about $2.7 trillion in its middle estimate (roughly 45% less), and $1.4 trillion with “extreme avoidance” (roughly 70% less). Lawrence Summers and Natasha Sarin argued from estate tax data that a wealth tax would raise as little as, or less than, the worst-case avoidance scenarios. The avoidance setting doesn’t affect income taxes, but high earners do find ways to pay less when income tax rates rise. That means the income sliders show somewhat more than would really be collected.
  • Wealth is a stock; income is a renewable flow: A wealth tax collects from the same pile every year, so high rates shrink the pile over time; if you apply a 25% tax to wealth, for instance, it would quickly deplete much of the pile. This page shows what the first year would look like.
  • Slider limits: The wealth sliders stop at 25%; the income sliders run from 5 points below current tax rates to 30 points above. These are choices I made for this page. They sit well above actual proposals, which included top wealth tax rates up to 8%, so you can test more extreme plans. The sliders stop where the simple math becomes too unreliable to be useful even as an illustration. A caution appears when a wealth slider passes 8% or an income slider passes 15 points.
  • Proposal costs: The costs in the proposal dropdown menu come from different groups and different years and aren’t adjusted to 2026 dollars. Ten-year estimates are divided by 10. Estimates of the same idea can differ widely depending on how the plan is designed and who’s counting. The source for each appears when you select it.
  • Corporate taxes: Each group’s current tax rate includes its share of corporate income taxes, which Treasury assigns mostly to the families who own company stock; the top 10% of families pay about 71% of them. There’s no separate corporate tax slider, but because a corporate tax increase would fall mostly on the top income groups, you can approximate one with those sliders. For simplicity, I also leave out other ways to raise money, such as tariffs, a national sales tax, or a carbon tax.
  • Deficit: $2.0 trillion for fiscal year 2026, from the Congressional Budget Office’s Monthly Budget Review, November 2026.
  • Income groups and current tax rates: U.S. Treasury, Office of Tax Analysis, Distribution Table 2025-001, the most recent the Treasury has published. It predates the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act tax changes, and incomes have increased since, so the income sliders slightly understate today’s dollars. “Federal taxes” includes income, payroll, corporate, excise, and estate taxes. Income thresholds are for a two-person family. Families are tax units, about 189 million in all.
  • Billionaires: 989 people with $8.4 trillion, from the Forbes list of March 2026, as reported by The Center Square. Other counts differ by date and method.
  • Wealthiest 0.1%: nearly $28 trillion in the second quarter of 2026, from the Federal Reserve’s Distributional Financial Accounts as analyzed by Fortune. The “rest of the 0.1%” figure subtracts the Forbes billionaire total, which mixes two sources and is approximate.
  • The book: Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick, The Everywhere Millionaire (Henry Holt, 2026). The 3 million and 13x figures are from the authors’ own summary; also see Planet Money’s coverage. Their group is defined by business ownership and wealth, so it doesn’t map onto a single slider here; most of its members likely fall in the top 10% by income (the top three income sliders), and only the richest are reached by the “Rest of the wealthiest 0.1%” slider. That placement is an estimate, not a figure from the book.
  • Whether any group should pay more is a question of values. This page only shows how much each choice raises.
